# How children learn languages, and how to design language-learning apps for kids safely

Method note (read first): WebFetch is blocked in this environment, so every finding below comes from WebSearch result summaries and snippets. I did not open any of the source pages in full. Treat numbers as "as reported in the search summary of the cited page". Where a claim rests on a weak or secondary page (blog, vendor, law-firm summary), it is marked. State of knowledge: as of 6 October 2026.

Evidence-strength tags used throughout:
- [MA] meta-analysis; [SR] systematic review; [RCT] randomised trial; [EXP] lab or quasi-experiment; [OBS] observational, survey or content analysis; [EXPERT] expert framework, guideline or position statement; [VENDOR] company claim; [LAW] statute, regulation or official regulator guidance; [LEGAL-COMM] law-firm or secondary legal commentary; [SECONDARY] blog or news summary of other work.
- [OLDER] means the finding is more than about 8 years old but has not been overturned. [SUPERSEDED] means newer guidance or law replaces it.

## 1. Age effects in L2 learning (critical/sensitive period, younger vs older learners in instructed settings, implicit vs explicit learning by age)

### Takeaway
Starting young brings an advantage in eventual attainment mainly under immersion and long exposure. In classroom-type (instructed) settings, older children and teens learn faster per hour, and the total amount of exposure matters more than starting age. Children are not purely "implicit" learners. Instruction type shapes how they learn, and short, age-appropriate explicit explanations help children from about 8 years old onwards.

### Cited Findings
- **Critical period for grammar, "big data" study** [OBS, large-scale, OLDER-2018 but still the main reference]: Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker (2018, *Cognition*) used 669,498 native and non-native English speakers and a computational model that separates current age, age at first exposure and years of experience. They estimated that grammar-learning ability stays high until about **17.4 years** and then declines steadily. It is "nearly impossible" to reach native-like grammar unless learning starts **by about age 10**. People who start between 10 and 18 still learn quickly but have less time before the decline. — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/126641); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6559801); [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/index%2Ephp/2018/cognitive-scientists-define-critical-period-learning-language-0501)
- **Older learners' rate advantage in instructed settings** [SR/narrative review of Muñoz's program, OBS longitudinal]: Muñoz's research reports three things. First, younger starters do better on long-term attainment, mainly in naturalistic or immersion conditions. Second, older learners have a rate advantage and catch up with early starters in the short to medium term. Third, "when the amount of instruction or exposure is controlled for, even after many years of study, older school learners are observed to outperform younger school learners after the same number of hours of instruction." — [ERIC: The Role of Age in Second Language Development](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1415576.pdf)
- **Early-language-learning research brief** [SR, EXPERT]: ACTFL's research brief says most studies of early instruction found no advantage for younger learners, or found one for older learners. Older starters progressed faster at first, but younger learners caught up in listening and speaking over time. "The overall number of hours of exposure was more important than the age at which the children started." — [ACTFL Early Language Learning Brief (PDF)](https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Documents/Early_Language_Learning_Brief.pdf); [ACTFL research brief page](https://www.actfl.org/research/research-briefs/the-impact-of-early-language-learning)
- **Implicit vs explicit learning by age** [EXP]: Lichtman (2016, *Journal of Child Language*) taught 40 children aged 8–9 and 40 adults an artificial mini-language under implicit or explicit training. The shift from implicit learning in childhood to explicit learning in adulthood "is not caused by age alone". The type of instruction has a significant influence on implicit vs explicit learning at any age. — [Cambridge Core PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/457C069A5339D656788E7E8D217B2A3A/S0305000915000598a.pdf/age-and-learning-environment-are-children-implicit-second-language-learners.pdf)
- **Explicit instruction and metalinguistic awareness in children** [EXP, single study, snippet only]: in a University of Minnesota study, children learned better with explicit-based procedures than with implicit-based ones. Outcomes depended on age, nonverbal cognition and metalinguistic awareness. — [UMN Conservancy](https://conservancy.umn.edu/items/f7a37448-5118-4a3b-912f-afa7d9765282)
- **DeKeyser's position** [EXPERT/theory]: declarative knowledge of rules helps learners proceduralise and automatise them. DeKeyser argues explicit learning is especially useful for adults and for simple, categorical L2 rules. — [Cambridge Core PDF (Lichtman, citing DeKeyser)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/457C069A5339D656788E7E8D217B2A3A/S0305000915000598a.pdf/age-and-learning-environment-are-children-implicit-second-language-learners.pdf)
- **Infant phonetic learning needs a live person** [EXP, OLDER-2003, foundational]: Kuhl, Tsao & Liu (2003) exposed 9-month-olds to Mandarin in 12 lab visits of 25 minutes. Infants with a live speaker learned the Mandarin phonetic contrasts. Infants exposed by DVD or audiotape scored no better than English-only controls. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2018 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02195/full); [UW News 2003](https://www.washington.edu/news/2003/07/14/social-interaction-plays-key-role-in-how-infants-learn-language-studies-show/)

### Inferences
- For Hevalo's diaspora children, "start early" matters most if it means **many hours of meaningful Kurdish input over years**, as in family life. Short daily app sessions alone will not recreate immersion. The app's best role for young children is to multiply and structure home exposure, not to replace it.
- For children of about 8–12, short, kid-friendly explicit explanations are supported (Lichtman; the UMN study), for example "in Kurmancî, *-ê* marks…". This suits the alphabet module and grammar tips, as long as they are brief and followed by practice.
- Older children and teens will progress faster per hour than 4–6-year-olds. Kids' tracks should not be benchmarked against adult or teen pacing.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the published critiques of Hartshorne et al.'s 17.4-year cut-off in this session. I believe later reanalyses dispute how sharp the decline is. Treat "17.4" as an estimate, not a fixed boundary.
- I found no study on age effects specific to Kurdish (Kurmanji or Sorani), or to heritage learners of Kurdish.
- I found no effect sizes for the instructed-setting rate advantage. The sources above describe it only qualitatively.

## 2. What works for young learners (play, songs/rhymes, stories and shared reading, TPR, repetition, multisensory input, contingent social interaction, parent co-use)

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence for young children concerns **interaction with a live, responsive person**, including through live video chat, and **shared book reading with a trained caregiver**. The effects are real but moderate, and they shrink against active controls. Songs and Total Physical Response (TPR) are popular and promising, but the evidence is weaker: mostly small quasi-experiments. Digital storybooks help when they add narration, animation and sound that match the story. Interactive "hotspots", games and dictionaries inside stories distract children.

### Cited Findings
- **Contingency rescues screen-based learning** [EXP]: toddlers aged 24–30 months learned novel verbs from live interaction and from socially contingent video chat, but not from non-contingent video (Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 2014). — [PMC3962808](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3962808)
- **Toddlers learn from FaceTime but not from pre-recorded video** [EXP]: Myers et al. (2017, *Developmental Science*) gave 60 children aged 1–2 a week of either real-time FaceTime chats or pre-recorded videos. The FaceTime group recognised the person they had "met" and learned new words and patterns. The pre-recorded group did not. From about 17 months, toddlers can interact and respond in real-time video chat. — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/new-study-of-toddlers-sheds-light-on-value-of-facetime-video-chat-as-meaningful-interaction); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/311683/)
- **The "video deficit"** [EXPERT, OLDER]: Anderson & Pempek (2005) coined the term. Kuhl et al. proposed that face-to-face social cues "attract infants' attention and motivate learning". — [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02195/full)
- **Shared book reading, caregiver interventions** [MA of 19 RCTs]: Dowdall et al. (2020) covered N = 2,594 children with mean ages of 1–6. Book-sharing interventions had **d = 0.41 on expressive language** and **d = 0.26 on receptive language**, and a large effect on caregiver book-sharing skill (**d = 1.01**). The effect on child language depended on dosage: low dosage had minimal impact. — [Hope repository](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/id/eprint/3756/); [Reading repository](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/80756)
- **Shared reading effects are smaller than often claimed** [MA]: Noble et al. (2019) found an overall **g = 0.194** (p = .002). Against active control groups the effect was **g = 0.028 (n.s.)**, and effects at follow-up were not significant (g = 0.139). Dialogic-reading techniques did not significantly moderate the effect. — [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3054189/)
- **Dialogic reading on expressive vocabulary** [MA, snippet only; source paper not identified with certainty, possibly Mol et al. 2008]: a meta-analysis of 9 studies (n = 322) reported a moderate effect of dialogic reading on expressive vocabulary (**d = .59**). The search summary returned this alongside the [Leiden repository item](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2899524/view). Verify before quoting.
- **Shared reading for English language learners** [MA, title only]: a meta-analysis exists of shared book reading on ELL language and literacy outcomes. I did not see its numbers. — [Scholar Commons (Univ. of South Carolina)](https://scholarcommons.sc.edu/sph_communication_sciences_disorders_facpub/89/)
- **Technology-enhanced storybooks** [MA, OLDER-2015, still the main synthesis]: Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015, *Review of Educational Research*) covered 43 studies and 2,147 children. Technology added a small benefit for story comprehension (**g+ = 0.17**) and expressive vocabulary (**g+ = 0.20**). **Multimedia features** (animated pictures, music, sound effects) helped. **Interactive features** (hotspots, games, dictionaries) distracted. Children from less stimulating home environments gained most from multimedia and were hurt most by interactive features. — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2872126/view); [SAGE PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)
- **Songs and TPR for preschool EFL** [EXP, quasi-experiment]: 72 Thai preschoolers aged 4–5 learned 12 words over 6 weeks through TPR, songs, or both. Both methods improved vocabulary acquisition, and the combination worked best. — [ERIC EJ1304648](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1304648.pdf)
- **Songs, systematic review** [SR]: a review in *System* (2024) found 60 intervention studies from 23 countries on songs and linguistic outcomes. The evidence "is not substantial or reliable enough to make any strong causal inferences about the effect of singing on vocabulary uptake." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0346251X24001325)
- **Songs, meta-analysis** [MA, abstract snippet only]: a meta-analysis of English songs on L2 lexical learning found effects moderated by publication status, song genre, control type, instructional duration, education level and learners' L1. I did not see the overall effect size. — [Taylor & Francis (Language, Culture and Curriculum 35(4))](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07908318.2021.2022684)
- **Joint media engagement (JME)** [EXPERT, OLDER-2011, framework]: Takeuchi & Stevens (Joan Ganz Cooney Center, 2011) defined JME. When children and adults use media together, learning is supported through shared meaning-making, links to prior knowledge, personal connections, and parents scaffolding with questions and new-word clarifications. Children learn more when parents co-view. — [The new coviewing (PDF)](https://www.thechildrensmediafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/02/Takeuchi-2011-The-new-coviewing.pdf); [International Journal of Communication 2017](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/4233/1919/0)
- **Spacing in children** [EXP, lab]: Vlach & Sandhofer (2012) found that spaced learning events improve children's long-term learning and help them acquire and generalise concepts. — [Learning Scientists blog summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/7/20-1)
- **Spacing and retrieval practice in real primary-school vocabulary lessons** [EXP, classroom; a null result]: Goossens et al. tested spaced and retrieval practice with real class vocabulary in grades 2, 3, 4 and 6. Long-lag repetition and retrieval practice showed no benefit on cued-recall or multiple-choice tests, "putting into question the practical value" of these in real primary vocabulary lessons. — [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/67956); [Goossens dissertation (PDF)](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/77635/Proefschrift-Nicole-Goossens.pdf)
- **Vendor efficacy claim, Lingokids** [VENDOR]: Lingokids cites a UC Davis study in which children using the app showed a "14% improvement in vocabulary compared to peers". I did not see the study design, sample, or whether it was independent or peer-reviewed. — [Lingokids blog](https://lingokids.com/blog/posts/efficacy-study-backs-lingokids-playlearning-methodology); [Lingokids research page](https://lingokids.com/research)

### Inferences
- **Most leverage for Hevalo:** features that put a live, responsive Kurdish speaker in the loop, such as a parent, grandparent or teacher, live or by synchronous video. This matters most for children under about 5. Pre-recorded content alone is weak for under-3s.
- **Coach the adult as well as the child.** In Dowdall the largest effect was on caregiver skill (d = 1.01), and child gains depended on dosage. A feature that teaches diaspora parents how to read and talk in Kurdish with the child may beat any child-only drill.
- **Story mode:** keep narration, music, sound and animation that match the story. Leave out tap-to-find hotspots, mini-games and pop-up dictionaries inside the story, especially for younger or less-advantaged children (Takacs).
- **Songs, rhymes and TPR** are low-risk, culturally rich (Kurdish *lorî* lullabies, folk songs, dance-movement words) and engaging. Present them as engagement and exposure tools, not as proven vocabulary accelerators.
- **Spaced repetition for young children:** do not over-engineer adult-style SRS drills. Recycle words across stories, songs and games at spaced intervals instead. Lab evidence supports spacing, and classroom drill evidence is mixed.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis on TPR effect sizes turned up. The evidence is small studies.
- No evidence was found on Kurdish-specific children's media or on L2 or heritage learning outcomes from apps in low-resource languages.
- Of Roseberry 2014 and Myers 2017, effect sizes were not available in the snippets.

## 3. Evidence-based criteria for children's educational apps; design for pre-readers and early readers

### Takeaway
Educational apps for children aged 3–8 show moderate average effects (**+0.31 SD**). The effects are biggest for preschoolers and for constrained skills such as letters, phonics and numbers, and smaller on standardised or broad measures. Most "educational" apps in the stores score low on the Four Pillars (active, engaged, meaningful, socially interactive). Pre-readers need audio-first, picture-led interfaces with large tap targets and no reliance on text.

### Cited Findings
- **Four Pillars framework** [EXPERT, OLDER-2015, still the reference framework]: Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh, Golinkoff, Gray, Robb & Kaufman (2015, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 16(1), 3–34) proposed that learning happens when children are **actively** involved ("minds-on", not just tapping), **engaged** with the learning goal rather than distracted, building **meaningful** links to their lives, and **socially interactive**. The fourth pillar sits within a context of a **learning goal**. — [PSPI PDF](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2015/09/Psychological-Science-in-the-Public-Interest-2015-Hirsh-Pasek-3-34.pdf); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100615569721); [Chartered College summary](https://my.chartered.college/research-hub/how-to-choose-high-quality-educational-apps-and-programmes-for-children/)
- **Most store "educational" apps score low** [OBS, content analysis]: Meyer et al. (2021) built a reliable coding scheme for the Four Pillars. They coded the top 100 children's educational apps on Google Play and Apple, plus 24 apps most played by preschoolers in a cohort study. Most scored low, and free apps scored lower than paid ones on some criteria (more ads and distractions). — [PubMed 35282402](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35282402/); [Penn State News](https://news.psu.edu/story/657763/2021/05/11/research/top-educational-apps-children-might-not-be-beneficial-promised); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/05/210510143915.htm)
- **Average effect of educational apps** [MA]: Kim, Gilbert, Yu & Gale (2021, *AERA Open*) covered 36 studies and 285 effect sizes. Rigorous studies averaged **+0.31 SD** on overall achievement, similar in math and literacy. Effects were **larger for preschool than K–3**, for **researcher-developed than standardised** outcomes, and for **constrained skills** (letter knowledge, phonics) than **unconstrained skills** (comprehension). — [Harvard CEPR](https://cepr.harvard.edu/resource/measures-matter-meta-analysis-effects-educational-apps-preschool-grade-3-childrens-0); [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1323865.pdf)
- **Newer quality appraisal** [OBS, 2025, title only]: a 2025 *Early Childhood Education Journal* paper appraised the quality of foundational-literacy apps ("Finding the Needle in the Haystack"). I did not see its findings. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-025-02066-8)
- **Age bands for UX** [EXPERT/UX research, via secondary summaries]: Nielsen Norman Group's children's research splits ages 3–5 (pre-readers), 6–8 (beginning readers) and 9–12 (moderately skilled readers). Children aged 3–5 tap and swipe and cannot use trackpads or keyboards. Children aged 6–8 can also manage simple clicks and keystrokes. From 9, children can drag, scroll and coordinate keyboard and mouse. Pre-reader designs need large pictures, instant audio and visual feedback, allowance for short attention spans and multiple simultaneous taps, and an awareness that young children cannot tell ads from content. — [NN/g video page](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/designing-children/); [Raw Studio summary](https://raw.studio/blog/designing-for-children/); [UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2020/01/ux-design-for-kids-key-design-considerations.php)
- **Audio-first design in practice** [VENDOR/engineering write-up]: Duolingo ABC (ages 3–8) "relies heavily on audio narration and animation". All instructions and feedback are spoken aloud and stories are read aloud. Narration is assembled from audio pieces (e.g., "The letter team" + [letter] + [letter] + "says" + [phoneme]). — [Duolingo blog: building Duolingo ABC for Android](https://blog.duolingo.com/a-good-read-building-duolingo-abc-for-android/); [Duolingo ABC "How we teach"](https://abc.duolingo.com/how-we-teach)
- **"Playlearning" design** [VENDOR]: Lingokids (ages 2–8) mixes games, videos, songs and puzzles and cites constructivism, socio-cultural theory and gamification. — [Lingokids research](https://lingokids.com/research)

### Inferences
- **Most measurable app effects are on constrained skills**: letter–sound mapping, decoding, single-word vocabulary. Hevalo's alphabet module (Kurmancî Latin and Soranî Arabic-based scripts, with recorded audio) is the natural core of a kids' track, and outcomes should be measured there.
- **Unconstrained skills** (conversation, comprehension, using Kurdish at home) will not come from the app alone. They need the social and family features described in section 5.
- Hevalo should **self-audit kids' content against the Four Pillars coding scheme** (Meyer et al. 2021) before launch and track it as a quality KPI.
- **Pre-readers (3–5):** no text-dependent instructions, typing, Wordle or typing races. **Early readers (6–8):** tap and simple keystrokes, short words, decodable text. **9–12:** the existing word games become appropriate.

### Gaps
- I could not open NN/g's primary articles. The specific touch-target sizes NN/g recommends for children could not be verified.
- Duolingo ABC and Lingokids have published no independent peer-reviewed efficacy data that I could find. Their claims are vendor claims.

## 4. Screen-time guidance, attention and session length, rewards and gamification effects on children, avoiding manipulative design

### Takeaway
Guidance has moved from hour limits toward the **quality, context and design** of media. The AAP's January 2026 policy statement frames children's media as a "digital ecosystem" designed for engagement and profit. The WHO still recommends no sedentary screen time for under-1s and at most 1 hour for 2–4-year-olds. Expected, tangible, engagement-contingent rewards measurably undermine intrinsic motivation. Manipulative design is pervasive in young children's apps. EU and UK regulators now explicitly name **streaks, autoplay, push notifications and nudges** as features to disable or avoid for minors.

### Cited Findings
- **AAP 2026 policy statement** [EXPERT, current; SUPERSEDES AAP 2016 "Media and Young Minds"]: "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents: Policy Statement" (Munzer et al.), *Pediatrics* 157(2): e2025075320, published 20 January 2026, with a companion technical report (doi 10.1542/peds.2025-075321). It uses a socioecological model covering the child, caregivers, the digital ecosystem and society. It observes that "most platforms are designed to boost engagement and profit—and not to support children's health and development," and makes recommendations for industry and policymakers as well as families. — [Pediatrics](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-Policy); [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world-AAP-releases-new-media-recommendations.aspx); [AAP News](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/34088/Beyond-screen-time-Policy-discusses-how-to?autologincheck=redirected)
- **How secondary sources describe the 2026 change** [SECONDARY, blogs; treat with caution]: the AAP "stopped leading with a single daily hour limit" and focuses on content quality, whether screens displace sleep and activity, and compulsive use. Some blogs say "no screens before 18 months and one hour of high-quality content daily for ages 2–5" was kept. These blogs disagree on exactly what was kept. — [Pairently](https://pairently.com/blog/aap-screen-time-guidelines-2026-parents-guide); [Jellie's App](https://jelliesapp.com/blog/aap-screen-time-guidelines/); [CHOC](https://health.choc.org/updated-aap-recommendations-for-screen-time/)
- **WHO 2019 guideline** [EXPERT, OLDER-2019 but current WHO position]: no sedentary screen time for children under 1. For ages 2–4, no more than 1 hour, and "less is better". — [WHO guideline PDF (IRIS)](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/bfce7d1e-43d8-4e28-ba1b-8f6cf9da2661/content); [AOA summary](https://www.aoa.org/news/clinical-eye-care/public-health/screen-time-for-children-under-5)
- **How much children actually use screens** [OBS, nationally representative US survey]: the 2025 Common Sense Census reports about **2.5 hours a day** for children aged 0–8. That is 49 minutes for under-2s, about 2.5 hours for ages 2–4 and more than 3 hours for ages 5–8. 40% have a tablet by age 2, and nearly 1 in 4 has a phone by age 8. Gaming time rose 65% in four years. Online video is now the top screen activity, mostly without educational value. — [Common Sense Media 2025 Census](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight)
- **Rewards undermine intrinsic motivation** [MA, OLDER-1999, still influential but contested]: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) covered 128 studies. Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (**d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28**). They also undermined self-reported interest (d = −0.15, −0.17 for engagement- and completion-contingent), as did all tangible and all expected rewards. — [Deci et al. 1999 (PDF)](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [SDT 2001 follow-up in education](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- **Gamification and learning** [MA, not child-specific]: Sailer & Homner (2020) found gamification effects of **g = .49** on cognitive outcomes (k = 19, N = 1,686), **g = .36** on motivational outcomes and **g = .25** on behavioural outcomes. Only the cognitive effect held up in high-rigour studies. — [Springer, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)
- **Leaderboards with children and students** [OBS/EXP, small studies; secondary summaries]: leaderboards motivate top-ranked students but can create peer pressure, anxiety and feelings of inadequacy for middle and bottom ranks. Authors suggest relative (nearby-rank) leaderboards and not publicly showing bottom ranks. "Several authors caution that gamification may undermine intrinsic motivation… particularly among younger audiences." — [ECGBL paper](https://papers.academic-conferences.org/index.php/ecgbl/article/download/1593/1652/6517); [Int. J. Serious Games](https://journal.seriousgamessociety.org/index.php/IJSG/article/download/794/569); [UniversityXP summary](https://universityxp.com/research/2023/11/30/exploring-the-inner-workings-of-the-leaderboard-for-use-in-educational-contexts)
- **Manipulative design is common in young children's apps** [OBS, cross-sectional]: Radesky et al. (2022, *JAMA Network Open*) studied apps used by children aged 3–5. **80% contained manipulative design**, and **65%** used it to prolong engagement. Examples include a character yelling "save me!" with a countdown at a pause, lures such as stickers or trophies for accumulating daily rewards, and roadblock ads that stay up 20 seconds before the "X" appears. This was more common in apps used by lower-SES children. — [JAMA Netw Open via Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/janop/fulltext/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641~prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in); [Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use)
- **A 2025 audit of children's apps** [OBS, preprint, title only]: "Playful but Persuasive: Deceptive Designs and Advertising Strategies in Popular Mobile Apps for Children" (arXiv 2512.17819). — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.17819)
- **Regulators name streaks and nudges** [LAW, guidance]: the EU DSA Article 28 guidelines (14 July 2025) call for features that encourage excessive use, such as **"streaks"**, read receipts, autoplay and push notifications, to be **disabled by default** for minors. They also call for protection against manipulative design (details in section 6). The UK ICO Children's Code bars nudge techniques that lead children to give unnecessary data or weaken privacy, and encourages "positive nudges" toward high privacy and wellbeing. — [Hunton summary of EU guidelines](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/european-commission-issues-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-minors); [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-6-the-european-commission-finalises-guidelines-on-the-protection-of-102kv4s); [ICO nudge techniques toolkit](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-and-services/audits/data-protection-audit-framework/toolkits/age-appropriate-design/nudge-techniques/)

### Inferences
- Hevalo's adult engagement loop (XP, streaks, leagues, Zêr, shop) is the kind of design that regulators and the AAP now scrutinise when children use it. A kids' mode should **remove or re-shape** these mechanics rather than reuse them unchanged.
- Rewards should be **informational and unexpected** ("You learned 5 new animal words!" with a surprise sticker) rather than **expected and engagement-contingent** ("log in daily for coins"). The latter is the pattern with the largest undermining effect (d = −0.40).
- **Target co-use and short sessions.** Children aged 5–8 already average more than 3 hours of screens a day, so Hevalo Kids should present itself as the "good" screen time: short, social, and ending with an offline activity.

### Gaps
- **Attention span and ideal session length by age:** I found no reliable quantified evidence, such as a meta-analysis or RCT, on the best length for app learning sessions by age. Popular rules like "2–3 minutes per year of age" are folk heuristics that I could not source. NN/g only says to "acknowledge short attention spans".
- **Streaks and children's learning:** I found no experimental evidence on how streaks specifically affect children. The regulatory stance rests on excessive-use risk, not on learning data.
- Deci et al. 1999 has long been disputed by Cameron & Pierce, and I did not retrieve that rebuttal literature this session. The child-specific sub-group effect sizes were not in the snippet.

## 5. Heritage children: family language use, grandparents, home language maintenance; family dashboards and co-learning

### Takeaway
How much a child uses the heritage language depends most strongly on how much the **parents (ideally both) use it at home**. "One parent, one language" is neither necessary nor sufficient. Parents' attitudes and the value they place on the language predict children's skills, and the field is shifting toward digital maintenance strategies. Toddlers can learn from and bond with relatives over live video chat. Kurdish heritage schooling exists in Sweden and Germany, which is a partnership opportunity.

### Cited Findings
- **Parental input patterns predict children's minority-language use** [OBS, large survey, OLDER-2007, still a landmark]: De Houwer (2007, *Applied Psycholinguistics* 28(3), 411–424) surveyed 1,899 families in Flanders with at least one parent speaking a non-majority language and a child aged 6–10. All children spoke the majority language, but not all spoke the minority language. Patterns where **both parents used the minority language and at most one used the majority language** had a high chance of success. **"One parent–one language" was neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition.** — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/parental-language-input-patterns-and-childrens-bilingual-use/821A5852222197491F4E7ABC8AA4B099); [Psychology Today (De Houwer/Grosjean)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/life-bilingual/201410/the-languages-you-speak-your-bilingual-child)
- **Family language policy (FLP) review** [SR, title and abstract only]: "Can family language policy predict linguistic, socio-emotional and cognitive child and family outcomes? A systematic review" (*J. Multilingual & Multicultural Development*). FLP sets the frame for child–caretaker interactions, and outcomes vary with family factors and the importance given to maintenance. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01434632.2020.1858302)
- **FLP research 2020–2024 and the digital shift** [SR/OBS, 2025–2026, snippets only]: a 2026 mapping review of FLP research on heritage maintenance. A 2025 paper on "the digital shift in parental strategies for heritage language maintenance". In both, children show stronger agency to keep the language where parents strongly advocate for it and use it. Generational gaps matter: older generations prioritise the heritage language, younger ones lean toward the dominant language. School monolingualism pressures families. — [Mapping FLP 2020–2024 (T&F 2026)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311886.2026.2615946); [Digital shift (T&F 2025)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01434632.2025.2491607); [Pakistan FLP study (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11922832/)
- **Parental value predicts skills** [OBS, title only]: "How Parents' Perceived Value of the Heritage Language Predicts Their Children's Skills" (*Languages* 2024, 9(3), 80). — [MDPI/DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages9030080)
- **Children's perspective** [OBS, qualitative, 2026, title only]: Gorter & Berardi-Wiltshire (2026), "Heritage Language Maintenance and family relationship dynamics: A children's perspective", *International Journal of Bilingualism*. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13670069241311547)
- **FLP and vocabulary across ages** [OBS, title only]: "Family language policy and vocabulary of bilingual children across different ages" (ScienceDirect, 2023). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2215039023000474)
- **Video chat for distant relatives** [EXP + EXPERT]: from about 17 months, toddlers can recognise family they know in real life on video chat and learn from live video interaction (Myers et al. 2017). McClure & Barr (2017) wrote on "Building Family Relationships from a Distance" through video chat. — [Newswise (Myers 2017)](https://www.newswise.com/articles/new-study-of-toddlers-sheds-light-on-value-of-facetime-video-chat-as-meaningful-interaction); [Kinedu blog summary](https://blog.kinedu.com/the-value-of-facetime-do-toddlers-learn-and-bond-with-people-over-video-chat)
- **Kurdish heritage schooling in Europe** [SECONDARY, academic blog and news]: Sweden was the first European country to teach Kurdish in schools. A teacher training programme in both Kurmanji and Sorani started in Stockholm in 1984. In Germany, Kurdish has been taught as *Herkunftssprachenunterricht* since 1993, with about **3,000 pupils in seven federal states**, mainly in Kurmanji. — [Language on the Move](https://languageonthemove.com/kurdish-heritage-language-education/)
- **KRG–diaspora initiative** [SECONDARY, news/opinion]: Kurdish is now taught as a formal subject in Sweden and Germany under a joint KDC–KRG initiative, with "thirty schools abroad" registered and "three thousand online students". — [Kurdistan24](https://www.kurdistan24.net/en/opinion/893463/kurdish-now-taught-as-formal-subject-in-sweden-and-germany-under-joint-kdc-krg-initiative)

### Inferences
- The single most valuable heritage-children feature is probably one that **raises the amount of Kurdish adults speak to the child at home**, not more child drill. That means parent-facing prompts, phrase cards and co-learning, because many diaspora parents are semi-speakers or schooled in another language.
- **Grandparents are the high-proficiency speakers** in many diaspora families, often in the region. A family circle with synchronous video-chat reading and play could turn them into contingent tutors, the condition under which young children actually learn from screens.
- **Parent dashboards** should show learning, not just time. They should suggest offline activities and be transparent to the child that a parent can see activity (ICO Standard 11, section 6).
- **Partnerships:** Kurdish heritage teachers in Sweden and Germany (and the KRG online programme) are an obvious B2B2C channel for a "teacher/class" mode. Class mode also solves the child-to-child social problem safely, since children connect only with known classmates.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis quantifying how much heritage-language exposure (hours per week) children need for maintenance. De Houwer's percentages were not in the snippet.
- I found no study on Kurdish heritage children's language maintenance in Europe beyond the descriptive and ethnographic pieces above. I also found no evaluation of grandparent video-chat interventions on heritage-language outcomes.
- I found no published effect data on parent dashboards in children's language apps.

## 6. Legal and safety requirements (COPPA incl. 2025 update, GDPR-K/age of digital consent, UK AADC and Online Safety Act, EU DSA, app-store kids rules) and implications for social features and data collection

### Takeaway
Since **22 April 2026**, the amended US COPPA rule requires separate parental consent for third-party disclosures, a written security programme, a written data-retention policy (no indefinite retention) and neutral age screens. In the EU, the digital age of consent ranges from **13 to 16** by country (16 in Germany, the Netherlands and Ireland; 15 in France; 14 in Spain and Austria; 13 in Sweden). The UK Children's Code and the EU DSA guidelines demand **high-privacy defaults**: private accounts, no contact from strangers, no profiling or geolocation by default, and streaks, autoplay and push notifications off. Open DMs, public feeds, friend discovery and free-text multiplayer chat are therefore the highest-risk Hevalo features for child users.

## 7. Implications for Hevalo

### Takeaway
A safe, effective Hevalo for children would not be the adult app with a cartoon skin. It would be a parent-managed **"Hevalo Zarok" (kids) mode** with these properties:
- Audio-first and picture-led for pre-readers, with phonics and alphabet at the core.
- Social only within a family circle or class.
- Built around live adults (parents, grandparents, heritage teachers) as contingent tutors.
- No engagement-maximising mechanics: no loss-aversion streaks, public leagues, timed offers, push notifications or third-party SDKs.
- Measured on learning (letter–sound mastery, retained words), not minutes.

### Cited Findings
Implications rest on the findings in sections 1–6, mainly:
- Contingent social interaction (Roseberry 2014; Myers 2017; Kuhl 2003).
- Caregiver-coached shared reading (Dowdall 2020 d = 0.41 expressive; caregiver d = 1.01; Noble 2019 caution).
- Multimedia over interactive story features (Takacs 2015).
- App effects concentrated in constrained skills and preschoolers (Kim et al. 2021, +0.31 SD).
- The Four Pillars (Hirsh-Pasek 2015; Meyer 2021).
- Reward undermining (Deci et al. 1999).
- Manipulative design prevalence (Radesky 2022).
- Home input patterns (De Houwer 2007).
- The legal requirements in section 6.

### Inferences

#### A. Account structure and age handling
1. **Neutral age screen at sign-up**: a birth month and year picker with no pre-filled value and no messaging that older users get more. Store year and month only. Route users to Child (under 13, or under the country's Article 8 age where consent is the lawful basis), Teen (13–17) or Adult defaults. Keep a server-side table of consent ages per country (DE/NL/IE 16, FR 15, ES/AT 14, SE 13, and so on), and verify the remaining countries.
2. **Parent-owned family account**: a parent creates child profiles with a nickname and preset avatar, and no child email, photo, full name or school. Children sign in with a picture-password or parent-device handoff. US users need verifiable parental consent through an FTC-accepted method. The 2025 rule allows text message plus a confirmation step.
3. **App-store age signals**: consume platform age-range signals where state law (Texas/Utah) requires them, and use only for compliance and delete immediately (Texas). Verify which APIs exist.
4. **Store listing:** keep the main Hevalo app out of Apple's Kids Category, since the adult social features are incompatible with it. Implement the kids mode to Kids-Category standards anyway: no third-party analytics or ads, and a **parental gate** before external links, the shop and any purchase. If Google Play target audience includes children, apply the Families policy (no AAID or other device IDs for child users, no non-certified ad SDKs).

#### B. Social features: redesign for minors
5. **DMs and friends**: **off** for child profiles. For teens, off by default with no friend suggestions or "people you may know" (DSA: account recommendations off by default for minors; Ofcom: no stranger contact by default). Replace them with a **family circle**, where parents invite grandparents, aunts and cousins by code, and a **class circle**, where a verified teacher shares a class code. Within circles, children send **pre-set Kurdish phrases, stickers and voice messages to family only** ("Silav dapîr!", "Ez te hez dikim"), not free text.
6. **Community feed**: child profiles cannot post publicly. Offer a separate, **pre-moderated kids' story shelf** of curated Kurdish stories and poems, ideally narrated. Child submissions (drawings, poems) go to the family circle, or to a moderated showcase only with parent approval. **Strip EXIF and location** from all images and never allow selfies or face photos from child accounts (ICO geolocation standard; COPPA's definition of personal information has covered photos and audio containing a child's image or voice since the 2013 rule. That is prior knowledge, not re-verified this session).
7. **Multiplayer games (Kahoot-style, Wordle battle, rhyme match)**: children join only private rooms by code (family or class), with **auto-generated Kurdish nicknames** (e.g., "Şêrê Şîn", "Gula Sor") and no free-text names or chat, only emote buttons. No public matchmaking for under-13s. For teens, public matchmaking only with free-text chat off by default.
8. **Dictionary**: keep it public, but give kids' mode a **filtered lexicon**. A Wiktionary-derived 400k list will contain vulgar and sexual entries. Remove external links from kids' entries, or put them behind the parental gate.

#### C. Engagement mechanics: child-appropriate versions
9. **Streaks**: off by default for minors (DSA guidelines). If offered, use **weekly goals with built-in rest days and automatic "freeze"**. Never use guilt or loss framing ("your streak will die!") and never send streak-threat notifications.
10. **Leagues and rankings**: **off for children**. Replace them with **cooperative family or class goals** ("our family learned 100 words this month") and personal-best progress. For teens, make leagues opt-in, show only nearby ranks, and never publicly show bottom ranks or relegation.
11. **XP, Zêr currency and shop**: in kids' mode, earn cosmetics only through **learning milestones**, such as mastering a letter or finishing a story, and present them as **surprise, informational celebrations**. Avoid "log in daily to collect" rewards, which are engagement-contingent and expected (Deci d = −0.40), and the lures documented by Radesky (accumulating daily rewards). Do not use countdown timers, limited-time offers, loot-box mechanics, or characters pleading to keep playing. Put a parental gate before any real-money purchase.
12. **Push notifications**: off by default for minors. Send a **weekly parent digest** instead, by email or in the parent's app, in the parent's UI language.
13. **Natural stopping points**: no autoplay into the next lesson for children. Each kids' session ends with a "done for today" screen and an **offline mission** ("Ask Dayê what this is called in Kurdish", "Sing the song at bedtime"). This answers the AAP 2026 emphasis on displacement and compulsive use. Exact lesson length is not evidence-based; a reasonable starting hypothesis is 3–7-minute units for ages 3–6, to be A/B-tested on retention, not time-on-app.

#### D. Pedagogy by age band
14. **Pre-readers (3–5)**: **audio-first**. Every instruction is spoken in Kurdish, with an optional spoken scaffold in the parent's UI language (de, sv/nl/en, tr, ar). Use picture–word matching, big tap targets, instant audio feedback, and no text-dependent prompts, typing or Wordle. Core content: Kurdish nursery rhymes and *lorî*, **TPR-style action games** where a character models "Destên xwe bilind bike!" and the child copies, with a parent confirming, and narrated animated picture-books with no hotspots or mini-games inside the story (Takacs).
15. **Early readers (6–8)**: make the **alphabet module the core**. Kurmancî's Latin (Hawar) script is highly letter–sound regular, which suits phonics, the "constrained skills" where apps show their largest effects (Kim 2021). Add decodable mini-stories built only from letters the child has already learned (Duolingo ABC-style composited audio: "Tîpa *ş* dibêje /ʃ/"). For Soranî, teach the positional letter forms explicitly with audio. Tap and simple keystroke games are fine at this age.
16. **Ages 9–12**: the existing Wordle, rhyming rounds, quiz and typing race become appropriate, in private rooms. Add **very short explicit grammar tips** (Lichtman 2016, and the UMN study's finding that explicit procedures beat implicit ones), followed immediately by practice.
17. **Recycling rather than drilling**: schedule target words to come back across songs, stories and games over days, which is spaced exposure. Do not rely on adult-style flashcard SRS for young children (Goossens' null classroom result).
18. **Pronunciation**: child-speech ASR is unreliable. One secondary report gives about **25% WER for Whisper on child speech versus about 3% for adults**, and fine-tuned models reach 8.7–9.2% on English child corpora ([Learning Agency](https://the-learning-agency.com/the-cutting-ed/article/how-speech-recognition-systems-struggle-with-childrens-voices/); [Cambridge MLMI thesis 2025](https://www.mlmi.eng.cam.ac.uk/files/2025-11/Pang-Automatic-2025-web.pdf); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10520979)). Kurdish child speech will be worse. So **no automatic pass/fail on children's pronunciation**. Use **"listen, record, compare"**, where the child replays their own audio next to the native recording, or parent-judged "say it to Dayê/Bavê". Process voice on-device or delete it after playback (COPPA retention and biometrics).
19. **No open-ended AI chatbot for children.** If an AI tutor is added for teens, it must disclose that it is AI, take no companion persona, remind users to take breaks (SB 243 requires disclosure and a break reminder every 3 hours for known minors), have self-harm escalation protocols, and **not train on minors' data**.

#### E. Family and heritage features (highest expected impact)
20. **Parent co-learning track**: many diaspora parents are semi-speakers. Offer a parent mini-course of **"Kurdish to say to your child"**, covering routines such as meals, bath, bedtime, praise and questions, with audio. Each week the dashboard suggests **3 phrases to use at home** (De Houwer: home input by parents is what drives children's minority-language use).
21. **Dialogic reading coach**: when a parent and child open a story together, show the parent (in the parent's UI language) 2–3 prompts per page in Kurdish, e.g. "Ev çi ye?", "Paşê çi dibe?", "Tu jî ... dikî?". This copies the caregiver-training component behind Dowdall's d = 1.01 caregiver effect and d = 0.41 child effect. Track dosage, since low dosage meant minimal impact.
22. **"Read with Dapîr/Bapîr" synchronous mode**: a family-only live session in which grandparent and child see the same picture-book page or game board at the same time over video or voice, with page turns mirrored. This makes the screen contingent, the condition under which toddlers learn from video (Myers 2017; Roseberry 2014). Invites must come from a parent, and no recording should happen by default.
23. **Family voice library**: grandparents and relatives record words, songs and stories in their own dialect (Serhedî, Botanî, Badînî, Hewlêrî…), shared only within the family circle. It gives the child emotional and identity motivation and exposure to dialects. Pair it with live sessions, since recorded voice alone is non-contingent. Get parental consent and set retention controls for these recordings.
24. **Parent dashboard**: show *what* was learned (letters mastered, words known, stories finished, songs learned), not just minutes. Suggest an offline activity each week. Show the child an **obvious "your parent can see your progress" indicator** (ICO Std 11).
25. **Teacher and class mode** for Kurdish heritage classes (Germany's *Herkunftssprachenunterricht*, about 3,000 Kurdish pupils in 7 states; Swedish *modersmål* teaching; the KRG diaspora online programme). Teachers create class codes, assign stories and alphabet units, and run private Kahoot-style games. This solves safe peer interaction and reaches children at scale.

#### F. Quality assurance and measurement
26. **Four Pillars audit**: score every kids' activity with the Meyer et al. (2021) Four Pillars coding before release (active, engaged, meaningful, socially interactive, plus a clear learning goal). Remove purely decorative tapping.
27. **Measure learning, not engagement**: run outcome checks such as letter–sound accuracy, picture-naming of target words a week later, and parent-reported Kurdish use at home. Report both constrained and unconstrained measures, since apps inflate the former (Kim 2021). Do not run A/B tests on children that optimise time-on-app.
28. **Compliance package before launching kids' mode**:
    - a COPPA-2025 privacy notice with a **written retention schedule** (e.g., delete child voice immediately, delete inactive child profiles after N months);
    - a written information security programme;
    - **no third-party SDKs** in child sessions;
    - a DPIA under GDPR;
    - an ICO Children's Code best-interests self-assessment;
    - an Ofcom children's risk assessment if UK children can reach any user-to-user features.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found on optimal session length by age, or on how streaks affect children's learning. Items 9 and 13 are reasoned defaults to test, not established findings.
- No Kurdish-specific child-learning or child-ASR data was found. Items 15 and 18 extrapolate from English-language research.
- Several legal points need confirmation by counsel before engineering commitments:
  - the full EU Article 8 country table;
  - DSA Article 19's small-enterprise exemption;
  - Online Safety Act scope for Hevalo's UGC features;
  - the platform age-signal APIs.
