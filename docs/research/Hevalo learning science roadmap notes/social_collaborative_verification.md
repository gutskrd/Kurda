## Omitted counter-evidence and caveats

1. **The "graded reading library" needs a bilingual lexicon first.** Today the feed has no level tags and no glosses, and the dictionary defines Kurdish words in Kurdish (row 12). Building German, Turkish, Arabic and English glosses for at least the most frequent ~5–10k lemmas is a prerequisite for recs 24–25. The notes treat it as already available.
2. **Too few users (the cold-start problem).** These features all need enough people online at the same time, split by dialect, level, time zone and age band: team modes, matched leagues, study buddies, conversation clubs and mentor matching. A small Kurdish-learner base, split further by Kurmancî, Soranî and Zazakî and by age gating, may not fill them. The notes mention sparse networks only under Gaps. Asynchronous formats (turn-based games, voice-note exchanges), family and class circles, and personal-best comparisons should come first. (Same point in motivation_gamification_verification §12.)
3. **Peer corrections can be wrong, or can shame a dialect.**
   - [background, unverified] Learner-to-learner interaction gives fewer, and sometimes inaccurate, corrections, and learners often don't take them up (Philp, Adams & Iwashita 2014; Adams 2007).
   - Kurdish has no single spelling norm that writers follow in practice. Peer "corrections" can impose one dialect's or one region's norms on another, which is the dialect shaming the notes warn about.
   - Corrections should be anchored to a curated reference, and the current dictionary cannot serve as one (row 12).
4. **Learner-written posts are risky reading material.** Unedited learner posts contain errors. Little research covers whether learners pick up their peers' errors from reading. Only curated or speaker-checked texts should enter the "readable for you" levelling and the spaced-review pipeline. The notes' editor shelf covers only part of this.
5. **Making goals public can backfire.** [background, unverified] Gollwitzer, Sheeran, Michalski & Seifert (2009, *Psychological Science*) found that announcing identity-related intentions reduced later effort; replication evidence is mixed. Harkin's "public" moderator is a between-study comparison, mostly from health studies. Shared recaps should report *completed progress*, not announced intentions, and should stay optional.
6. **The rewards literature is contested.** [background, unverified] Cameron & Pierce (1994) and Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) found harm mainly from expected, tangible, task-noncontingent rewards, and no harm from verbal praise. This still supports "no Zêr per post". It also means performance-contingent recognition ("featured story") is low-risk.
7. **On average, competition is mixed rather than harmful.** [background, unverified]
   - Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth (2016, *RER*) reported that collaborative team competition and single-player non-competitive games outperformed single-player competitive games.
   - Chen, Shih & Law (2020, *ETR&D*) reported a small-to-moderate positive average effect of competition in digital game-based learning.
   - The harm is concentrated in low-ranked learners (logged leaderboard snippets).
   - So opt-in competition with relative ranks is reasonable for teens and adults. The notes' framing ("that is where the effect sizes point") overstates how clear-cut this is.
8. **Closer evidence than the classroom literature exists.** [background, unverified] Computer-supported collaborative learning meta-analyses (e.g., Chen, Wang, Kirschner & Tsai 2018, *RER*) found collaboration beat individual learning on knowledge gains in technology-mediated settings. That is closer to Hevalo than the face-to-face Johnson & Johnson literature. It is a lead worth retrieving.
9. **The video-chat evidence comes from toddlers.** [background, unverified] Roseberry et al. 2014 (24–30 months) and Myers et al. 2017 (12–25 months) used small samples and taught novel words or patterns. Carrying this over to grandparents teaching Kurdish to school-age children is plausible but untested.
10. **Volunteer models are fragile and need safeguarding.**
    - The notes' own Duolingo 2021 example shows volunteer-built content is hard to sustain. Volunteer moderators and hosts burn out [background: Matias 2019, "The civic labor of volunteer moderators online"].
    - Mentoring schemes usually require vetting [background: e.g., UK DBS checks for regulated activity with children]. The "adults only, no 1:1 with unrelated minors" rule in rec 34 is the right floor.
    - Harassment on open language-exchange apps is still unsourced in the notes.
11. **The "AI rehearsal" step rests on weak ground.**
    - "Chatbot practice is less anxiety-provoking than peers" comes from one summary sentence in a logged search. The search summary does not clearly tie that sentence to the cited System 2025 article, and the data are self-reported perceptions.
    - Kurdish LLM quality is uneven. The only logged evaluation (KurdEval: 5 Soranî prompts, 3 models) is informal, not a benchmark.
    - There is no Kurmancî speech pipeline (speech_audio_tech).
12. **Kurdish political and legal risk in the feed.** Users in Turkey, Iran or Syria may face real-world risk from what they post, not only from moderation outcomes. Default privacy, the option to post without real names, and careful handling of location and political content need legal review in those jurisdictions, beyond the German, Turkish, Iraqi and EU review the notes propose.
13. **The engagement-vs-learning gap applies to every social feature.** Rec 33's randomised rollout, with delayed-recall outcomes, is the right fix. Its power will be limited at Hevalo's scale (see adaptive_assessment_verification on power), so pre-registered, longer tests are needed.
