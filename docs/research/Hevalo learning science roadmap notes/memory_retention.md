## Q1. What do meta-analyses say about spacing, retrieval practice (testing), interleaving and productive recall vs recognition (effect sizes, boundary conditions, age, children)?

### Takeaway
Spacing and retrieval practice are the two best-supported levers: in L2 learning specifically, spacing gives g ≈ 0.58 (immediate) to 0.80 (delayed tests), retrieval practice beats restudy by g ≈ 0.50–0.61, and spacing *the retrievals* gives g ≈ 0.74 over massed retrieval; both work in primary-school children. Interleaving is **not** a universal win: g ≈ 0.42 overall, but blocking beat interleaving for word materials (g = −0.39); it helps when learners must discriminate similar categories (e.g., grammar forms). Productive (L1→L2) retrieval transfers to receptive knowledge much better than the reverse.

### Cited Findings

**Spacing (distributed practice)**
- [MA][Current] Kim & Webb (2022, *Language Learning*), the main L2 spacing meta-analysis: 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments, N = 3,411. Spacing had a medium-to-large effect: **g = 0.58 on immediate posttests, g = 0.80 on delayed posttests**. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358406370_The_Effects_of_Spaced_Practice_on_Second_Language_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis)
- [MA] Kim & Webb (2022) boundary conditions: shorter spacing was as good as longer spacing on immediate tests but **worse on delayed tests**; **equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent**; effect-size variability was explained by learning target, number of sessions, type of practice, activity type, feedback timing and retention interval. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479)
- [MA] Kim & Webb (2022) moderator: studies with **immediate feedback** showed a large spacing effect, **g = 1.04, 95% CI [0.59, 1.49]** [snippet-only; the comparison condition for this moderator was not in the snippet]. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358406370_The_Effects_of_Spaced_Practice_on_Second_Language_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis)
- [RCT/Exp, very large] Cepeda et al. (2008), >1,350 participants, study gaps up to 3.5 months, final tests up to 1 year later: the **optimal gap shrinks as a proportion of the retention interval — ~20–40% of a 1-week retention interval, ~5–10% of a 1-year interval** (often summarized as the "10–20% ratio rule"). — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0kp5q19x/qt0kp5q19x_noSplash_aedacd613d87c6d68878ac7ba09fdf0b.pdf?t=lnry4n); [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/temporal-spacing-and-learning)
- [MA] Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006, *Psychological Bulletin*) is the classic distributed-practice meta-analysis [its size/effect numbers were not in any snippet I retrieved — see Gaps]. — [York U page](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html)
- [MA] Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021, *Educational Psychology Review*): 29 studies / 39 effect sizes; **spaced retrieval practice beat massed retrieval practice, g = 0.74**; combining spacing + retrieval is better than either alone; **no clear evidence that expanding schedules beat uniform spacing**. — [LSCP PDF](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339735899_A_meta-analytic_review_of_the_benefit_of_spacing_out_retrieval_practice_episodes_on_retention)
- **[Superseded/Contested]** The older belief (from Landauer & Bjork-era work) that *expanding* schedules are inherently superior is not supported by the two meta-analyses above (Kim & Webb 2022; Latimier et al. 2021), which both find equal ≈ expanding. Modern SRS still expand intervals, but because memory stability grows, not because "expanding" is magic. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479); [LSCP PDF](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf)
- [MA] A 2025 *Educational Psychology Review* meta-analysis by Murray et al. on "the effectiveness of spacing and …" appeared in results [title truncated; content not retrieved]. — [PDF link](http://aidanhorner.org/papers/Murrayetal_EdPsychReview_2025.pdf)

**Retrieval practice / testing effect**
- [MA] Rowland (2014): 159 effect sizes from 61 studies, **testing vs restudy g = 0.50**; stronger when retrieval was more effortful, when material was more complex, and **when feedback was given**; larger effects for free recall than recognition formats. — [Frontiers 2026 summary citing Rowland](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1727423/full); [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- [MA] Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017): 272 effect sizes, 118 articles, 15,427 participants; **practice tests beat other learning conditions, g = 0.61**. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315706448_Rethinking_the_Use_of_Tests_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Practice_Testing)
- [MA] Yang et al. (2021, *Psychological Bulletin*): 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students; **classroom quizzing g ≈ 0.50 (0.499)**, across educational levels and from facts to problem-solving. — [Gwern-hosted PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- [MA, systematic review] Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021, *Ed Psych Review*): 49 effect sizes, 5,374 participants in real schools/classrooms; **57% showed medium or large benefits**; benefit held across education levels, content, delays, retrieval/final-test formats and feedback timing. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350049741_Retrieval_Practice_Consistently_Benefits_Student_Learning_a_Systematic_Review_of_Applied_Research_in_Schools_and_Classrooms); [Berklee repository](https://remix.berklee.edu/faculty-works/12/)

**Children**
- [RCT/Exp] Karpicke et al. (2016): 88 children (mean age ~10) showed **robust retrieval-practice effects, independent of reading comprehension and processing speed**. — [PMC4786565](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4786565/)
- [RCT/Exp] Primary-school children (8–12) improved fact recall after retrieval practice at 4 days, replicated at 1 and 5 weeks. — [PMC3827082](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3827082/)
- [RCT/Exp] Vlach & Sandhofer (2012): 5–7-year-olds given spaced science lessons showed better generalization for simple and complex concepts; Vlach, Sandhofer & Kornell (2008) found spacing benefits in young children's memory and category induction. — [Vlach & Sandhofer 2012 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-babytalk/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Sandhofer-CD-2012.pdf); [Vlach et al. 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027708001753)
- [RCT/Exp] Vlach et al. (2019) "To mass or space? Young children do not possess…" (title indicates young children lack accurate beliefs about spacing) [snippet-only, content not read]. — [Vlach Lab PDF](https://vlachlab.education.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/372/2026/03/VlachEtAl2019.pdf)
- [RCT/Exp] Children with developmental language disorder: retrieval practice with direct feedback and spaced trials increased word-learning efficiency; expanding retrieval offered at most modest extra benefit over spacing per se. — [PMC11087082](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11087082/); [PMC11365034](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11365034/)
- [MA] Uchihara et al. (2019): **age** moderated the effect of encounter frequency on incidental vocabulary learning [direction not in snippet]. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330774796_The_Effects_of_Repetition_on_Incidental_Vocabulary_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Correlational_Studies)

**Interleaving**
- [MA] Brunmair & Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin*): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples; **overall interleaving effect g = 0.42**; paintings g = 0.67; math g = 0.34; expository texts non-significant; **for word materials, blocking beat interleaving (g = −0.39)**; interleaving helps more when categories are similar to each other (high between-category similarity), less similar within category, and more complex. — [Würzburg PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Similarity-matters:-A-meta-analysis-of-interleaved-Brunmair-Richter/bb5392e8eaf53a38cc0d147f301cce74cecb4436)
- [RCT/Exp] Nakata & Suzuki (2019): interleaved practice of L2 grammar produced **more errors during training but better 1-week delayed performance** than blocked practice. — [Oxford ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:7096acb5-f759-4fe9-a06a-d6a0e20f75d0/files/ssf268575c); [ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1425968.pdf)
- [RCT/Exp, 2025–26] Xiong, *Modern Language Journal*: interleaving effects in L2 grammar **vary across grammatical patterns and cue reliability** [title-level only]. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/modl.70082)
- [MA/systematic review] Firth et al. (2021) systematic review of interleaving as a concept-learning strategy. — [BERA/Wiley](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rev3.3266)

**Generation / productive vs receptive retrieval; intentional vs incidental**
- [RCT/Exp series] Nakata's flashcard research: **receptive retrieval (L2→L1) gives large receptive gains but small productive gains; productive retrieval (L1→L2) gives relatively large gains in receptive knowledge as well**. Retrieval *frequency* matters more than mere number of encounters. — [Nakata 2020 Routledge Handbook chapter](https://howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Nakata-2020-The-Routledge-Handbook-of-Vocabulary-Studies_Part2.pdf); [Nakata 2013 dissertation](https://www.academia.edu/4557533/Nakata_T_2013_Optimising_second_language_vocabulary_learning_from_flashcards_Unpublished_doctoral_dissertation_Victoria_University_of_Wellington_New_Zealand)
- [Expert/Exp] Rowland (2014) and later syntheses: more effortful retrieval formats (free recall) yield larger testing effects than recognition. — [Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1727423/full)
- [MA] Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020, *Modern Language Journal*), meta-analysis of flashcards, word lists, writing and fill-in-the-blanks: gains on **delayed meaning-recall ≈ 39.4% and delayed form-recall ≈ 25.1%**; activity gains ranged **18.4%–77.0% (immediate) and 23.9%–73.4% (delayed)** — large variation by activity. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346001854_How_Effective_Are_Intentional_Vocabulary-Learning_Activities_A_Meta-Analysis); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12671)
- [MA] Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019): frequency of encounters and incidental vocabulary learning correlate **r = .34 (≈12% shared variance)**; moderated by age, prior vocabulary, spacing, visual support, engagement, test format. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330774796_The_Effects_of_Repetition_on_Incidental_Vocabulary_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Correlational_Studies)
- [MA] Uchihara et al. (2023, *Language Teaching*) meta-analysis "How effective is second language incidental vocabulary learning?" [figures not retrieved]. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-effective-is-second-language-incidental-vocabulary-learning-a-metaanalysis/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C)
- [Expert] Suzuki, Nakata & DeKeyser (2019, *MLJ* special issue "Optimizing second language practice in the classroom"): frame L2 practice as **desirable difficulty** — retrieval after a delay, discriminating similar forms, generating rather than recognizing — balanced with **transfer-appropriate** practice resembling real language use. — [EdUHK event page](https://www.eduhk.hk/ele/sc/event/optimizing-second-language-practice-repetition-is-important-but-how-to-repeat-is-important); [Recercat: lag effects in grammar learning](https://www.recercat.cat/handle/2445/216425)

### Inferences
- For a vocabulary app, the rough "stack" of effect sizes is: retrieval vs restudy (~0.5–0.6) and spacing (~0.6–0.8 on delayed L2 tests) are largest and additive (spaced retrieval ~0.74 over massed retrieval). These dominate interleaving (~0.4, and negative for word-type items).
- Interleaving should be targeted: use it for confusable grammar forms, letters and sounds; do not force it for brand-new vocabulary items.
- Because productive recall trains both directions while receptive recall does not, the "harder" production direction gives more value per retrieval. Recognition-only (multiple choice) should be a scaffold, not the end state.
- The child evidence supports the same principles (retrieval + spacing work from ~5–10 years), but children do not know spacing helps, so the app, not the child, has to do the scheduling.

### Gaps
- Cepeda et al. (2006) pooled numbers (e.g., number of studies/assessments, optimal lag tables) were not visible in any snippet.
- No meta-analysis specifically on retrieval practice for **L2 vocabulary in children** was found; child evidence is from individual experiments, mostly with L1 facts or science concepts.
- Direction of the age moderator in Uchihara et al. (2019) not retrieved.
- No research found on Kurdish (Kurmanji or Sorani) specifically; all evidence comes from other L2s (mostly English as L2, Japanese L1 learners in Nakata's work).

## Q2. How do SRS algorithms compare (Leitner, SM-2, Duolingo HLR, FSRS v4–6, 2024–2026 models), and what data does an app need to run FSRS?

### Takeaway
On the largest open benchmark (Anki review logs, tens of thousands of users, >10^9 reviews), FSRS-6 (21 parameters) predicts recall much better than HLR and far better than SM-2; FSRS-6 beats FSRS-5 and FSRS-4. Neural sequence models (LSTM, and especially RWKV trained across users) beat FSRS-6 by a wide margin in prediction, but are heavier. FSRS needs only a per-item review log: item id, timestamp, a 1–4 grade (or pass/fail), and ideally state and duration.

### Cited Findings
- [Expert/history] Duolingo's first scheduler was Leitner-style; Settles & Meeder (2016, ACL) report **HLR's error was roughly half of Leitner's**, and **>45% error reduction vs several baselines** in predicting recall, trained on **13 million Duolingo learning traces**. — [Duolingo research PDF](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/P/P16/P16-1174.pdf)
- [Vendor, peer-reviewed] HLR A/B test at Duolingo: **+9.5% daily retention for practice sessions, +1.7% for lessons, +12% for overall activity** in the HLR group. — [ACL preview PDF](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn)
- [Bench][Current] open-spaced-repetition **srs-benchmark**: dataset from **~20,000 Anki users, ~1.7 billion reviews** [snippet-only]. — [GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
  - Conflicting/older figure: Anki's default FSRS parameters were fitted on **~727 million reviews from 10,000 users** (earlier dataset version). — [Wikipedia: Anki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anki_(software))
- [Bench] Results reported for the benchmark "without same-day reviews" (lower log loss and RMSE = better; higher AUC = better) [all snippet-only from summaries of the README/Expertium page]:
  - **FSRS-6**: log loss **0.3460 ± 0.0042**, RMSE(bins) **0.0653 ± 0.0011**, AUC **0.7034 ± 0.0023**, 21 parameters.
  - **FSRS-5**: log loss **0.3560**.
  - **FSRS v4**: log loss **0.3726 ± 0.0048**, RMSE(bins) **0.0838 ± 0.0014**.
  - **HLR**: log loss **0.4694 ± 0.0074**, RMSE(bins) **0.1275 ± 0.0019**.
  - **LSTM** (8,869 params): log loss **0.3332 ± 0.0041**.
  - **RWKV-Instant** (~2.7M params): log loss **0.2773 ± 0.0036**; RWKV beats FSRS-6 on all three metrics "by a very big margin". **RWKV is trained on 5,000 users and evaluated on another 5,000 (not per-user)**, whereas FSRS etc. are optimized per user.
  — [srs-benchmark GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark); [Expertium benchmark page](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html); [DEV note on FSRS-6](https://dev.to/monruho/notes-on-fsrs-6-a-small-experiment-in-residual-calibration-5971)
- [Bench, older/unweighted run] **SM-2**: log loss **0.71 ± 0.013**, RMSE(bins) **0.199 ± 0.0021**; HLR in the same run 0.456 / 0.124 [snippet-only; from a different (unweighted, older) version of the benchmark than the FSRS-6 numbers, so not directly comparable]. — [srs-benchmark GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark); [ankitects/fsrs-benchmark](https://github.com/ankitects/fsrs-benchmark)
- [Expert/docs] FSRS is based on a variant of the **DSR (Difficulty, Stability, Retrievability)** memory model; integrated into **Anki 23.10 (2023)** as an optional scheduler, with parameters computable inside Anki. — [Wikipedia: Anki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anki_(software)); [Anki 23.10 changes](https://changes.ankiweb.net/changes/23.10.html)
- [Expert/docs] **FSRS-5** uses same-day reviews for training (not evaluation); **FSRS-6** improves same-day review handling and adds an **optimizable parameter controlling the flatness of the forgetting curve**, so curve shape differs per user; 21 trainable parameters. — [fsrs-optimizer GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-optimizer); [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
- [Peer-reviewed, Vendor-deployed] Ye, Su & Cao (KDD 2022, MaiMemo): memory model with Markov property built on **220 million** learners' memory logs; scheduler (**SSP-MMC**) provably minimizes review cost via stochastic shortest path; deployed in the language-learning app MaiMemo. This line of work is the academic basis of FSRS. — [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/kdd/YeSC22.html); [HIT Scholar](https://scholar.hit.edu.cn/en/publications/a-stochastic-shortest-path-algorithm-for-optimizing-spaced-repeti/)
- [Peer-reviewed workshop, 2026] Ilídio, Ghinis, Said Metwaly, Vens, Cornillie & Gharahighehi, "Revisiting Half-Life Regression for Explainable and Personalized Spaced Repetition in Duolingo" (PMLR 339:217–227, 2026): **mixed-effects HLR** (learner, lexeme and context random effects) feeding a **random forest**; improved prediction over original HLR while staying explainable [magnitude not in snippet]. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v339/ilidio26a.html)
- **Data FSRS needs** [Expert/docs]: a review log per review with **card_id, review_time (ms timestamp), review_rating (1–4: Again/Hard/Good/Easy), review_state (learning phase), review_duration**. — [fsrs-optimizer GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-optimizer); [DeepWiki summary](https://deepwiki.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-optimizer)
- **How much data** [Expert/docs, snippet-only]: pretraining of initial stabilities starts at **8 reviews**, full optimization at **64 reviews**; recommendation to accumulate **~1,000 reviews** with default weights before optimizing; an Anki issue argued the minimum could be reduced to 16. — [fsrs-optimizer GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-optimizer); [Anki issue #3094](https://github.com/ankitects/anki/issues/3094); [Anki forum: how many reviews](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/how-many-reviews-for-accurate-optimization/53320)
- [Expert] FSRS implementations across languages and apps are catalogued in **awesome-fsrs**; an open-source **srs-simulator** compares schedulers and study behaviours at scale. — [awesome-fsrs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs); [srs-simulator](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-simulator)
- [Vendor] Language apps now ship FSRS-6 (e.g., BrioVocab, a French app recalculating per-learner parameters) [aggregator snippet]. — [awesome-fsrs](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs)

### Inferences
- **[Superseded]** SM-2 (1987-era fixed ease-factor formula, still Anki's legacy default and the basis of many clones) is clearly superseded for prediction; Leitner boxes even more so. HLR (2016) is superseded by FSRS on the open benchmark, although the 2026 mixed-effects HLR shows HLR-style feature models remain a live option where item/learner features (lexeme, part of speech, context) matter — exactly the language-app case.
- The RWKV result implies that a **population-level model trained across many users** can beat per-user optimization. For a small/new app this cuts both ways: Hevalo will not have Anki-scale data for years, so start with FSRS defaults and per-user optimization, and treat a cross-user neural model as a later upgrade once logs accumulate.
- Benchmark wins are about *prediction* (calibration/log loss), not proven learning gains. The only causal evidence for better scheduling → better outcomes in a language app is Duolingo's HLR A/B test (engagement and retention metrics).

### Gaps
- Could not read the benchmark README directly; exact dataset version, metric weighting and whether SM-2 appears in the current FSRS-6 table could not be confirmed.
- No retrieved evidence on an "FSRS-7" or any post-FSRS-6 release as of Oct 2026 (absence in search results, not confirmation it does not exist).
- No public A/B evidence that FSRS improves actual learning outcomes vs SM-2 in a language app (as opposed to prediction accuracy/workload).
- Duolingo's current production model ("Birdbrain") details are only available via aggregator blogs; no primary source retrieved.

## Q3. Optimal retention targets, review-load trade-offs, and scheduling reviews for words seen in games/reading rather than flashcards

### Takeaway
The usual target is ~90% predicted recall at review time; above 90% review workload rises steeply and above ~97% becomes overwhelming. The "most learned per minute" target is user-specific and is found by simulation. There is no established method for crediting words seen in games or reading; the evidence says passive encounters help (r ≈ .34) but retrieval counts more than exposure, so in-game *retrievals* should be logged as graded reviews while passive exposures should be logged but weighted less.

### Cited Findings
- [Expert/docs] Anki manual: **desired retention** is the proportion of cards recalled when due and is the most important FSRS setting; **above 90% workload increases very quickly; above 97% it can be overwhelming**; permissible range 0.70–0.97 (0.70–0.99 in Anki 23.10.1+). — [Anki manual: deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=learning); [fsrs4anki tutorial](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md)
- [Expert/docs] Anki's "Compute minimum recommended retention (CMRR)" simulated starting from 0 cards and found the desired retention that maximizes material learnt per unit time, using the user's parameters and **review timings**; users may set higher retention if they accept more study time. — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=learning); [Anki forum: what optimal retention means](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/clarify-what-optimal-retention-means/42803)
- [Expert/vendor blogs] FSRS is commonly run with a default target of ~90% retention. — [Migaku blog](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/spaced-repetition-in-2026-how-it-actually-works); [Fluentcards](https://fluentcards.org/blog/fsrs-spaced-repetition-algorithm/)
- [RCT/Exp] Cepeda et al. (2008): the ideal gap depends on how long you need to remember (shorter fraction for longer horizons) — the retention horizon, not only a % target, drives scheduling. — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0kp5q19x/qt0kp5q19x_noSplash_aedacd613d87c6d68878ac7ba09fdf0b.pdf?t=lnry4n)
- [MA] Incidental exposure: frequency of encounters ↔ incidental vocabulary learning **r = .34**; spaced encounters and engagement moderate it. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330774796_The_Effects_of_Repetition_on_Incidental_Vocabulary_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Correlational_Studies)
- [RCT/Exp] Nakata: **retrieval frequency matters more than number of encounters**. — [Nakata 2020 handbook chapter](https://howtoeigo.net/research/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Nakata-2020-The-Routledge-Handbook-of-Vocabulary-Studies_Part2.pdf)
- [RCT/Exp] Vocabulary learning during reading: work comparing contextual inference vs retrieval opportunities during reading (PMC9285746) [title-level only]. — [PMC9285746](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9285746/)
- [RCT/Exp] Kim & Webb (2023, *MLJ*) tested whether spacing works the same for **fill-in-the-blanks vs flashcards** (i.e., whether activity type changes spacing effects) [conclusion not in snippet]. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/modl.12879)
- [Vendor] Duolingo: Practice Hub content is generated from the learner's recent personal activity in lessons; the course path ordering itself is grounded in spaced repetition; mistakes are re-asked at the end of a lesson. — [Duolingo Practice Hub guide](https://blog.duolingo.com/guide-to-duolingo-practice-hub/); [Duolingo: spaced repetition](https://blog.duolingo.com/spaced-repetition-for-learning)
- [Vendor/peer-reviewed] HLR was trained on word-level "learning traces" from Duolingo exercises (not separate flashcards), showing a word-level memory model can be fed by in-lesson practice. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/P/P16/P16-1174.pdf)
- [Expert/vendor blog] Common "mining" workflow: meet a word in content → save it with its original sentence → review it in the SRS → meet it again in content; the SRS bridges natural encounters. — [Migaku blog](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/spaced-repetition-in-2026-how-it-actually-works)

### Inferences
- A sensible default for adults is 0.85–0.90 desired retention, with a "light" mode (~0.80) for casual learners and children to keep daily load and frustration low, and a per-user simulation to show the time cost of higher targets.
- Game events that require the learner to *produce or recognize* a word (Wordle guesses, typing race, quiz answers, rhyme match) are retrievals and can feed the scheduler as graded reviews; reading a story or looking up a word is an exposure. Because FSRS is defined on graded retrievals, exposures are better stored as a separate signal (e.g., feature for an HLR-style or neural model, or a small "nudge" to due dates) than as full reviews.
- Recognition-format successes (multiple choice, four-option quiz with guessing) are weaker evidence of memory than typed recall, so grade mapping should be format-aware (e.g., MCQ correct ≈ "Hard"/"Good", typed correct fast ≈ "Good"/"Easy").

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on **how to credit implicit/incidental exposures inside an SRS model**; this is open design territory.
- No evidence retrieved on optimal retention targets specifically for children or for language (vs medical) decks.
- Current Anki versions may have changed or replaced the CMRR feature; not verified.

## Q4. Sleep and consolidation, session length and distribution (massed vs distributed), and the forgetting curve in apps

### Takeaway
Sleep after learning helps new words (g ≈ 0.50 vs wake; recall g ≈ 0.57), and children may benefit even more than adults, so "learn today, retrieve tomorrow" should be the default first interval. Within a session, more retrievals per new word (5–7 beat 1–3) and longer within-session gaps (larger sets) improve retention; more, shorter, distributed sessions beat fewer, massed ones. The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve replicates, and FSRS-6 now lets its shape vary per learner.

### Cited Findings
- [MA] Schimke et al. (2021, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): 25 studies, 42 outcomes (29 between-group comparisons, 1,396 participants, healthy adults): **sleep > wake for novel word learning, g = 0.50**; **recall g = 0.57, recognition g = 0.52, lexical integration g = 0.39**. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-021-01980-3); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354746737_The_effect_of_sleep_on_novel_word_learning_in_healthy_adults_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis)
- [RCT/Exp, boundary condition] Schimke et al. (2024, *QJEP*): **no effect of sleep on contextual word learning** in younger adults. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/17470218231179459)
- [RCT/Exp] Children vs adults (Sci Reports 2020): 30 children (7–12) and 34 adults; **children who slept maintained or slightly improved recall, adults who slept declined** — consistent with more slow-wave sleep in children [snippet-only]. — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-66880-3)
- [RCT/Exp] Sleep consolidation improved children's word learning from storybooks ("Goodnight book"). — [PMC3941071](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3941071/)
- [Obs/Exp] Age-related changes in sleep-dependent word consolidation, and "rich get richer" (larger existing vocabulary → more consolidation) findings. — [PMC8771760](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8771760); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763416305784)
- [MA] Kim & Webb (2022): **number of sessions** moderated L2 spacing effects; longer spacing wins on delayed tests. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479)
- [RCT/Exp] Nakata (2017, *SSLA*): learners retrieving each of 16 L2 words **5 or 7 times** in one session (L1→L2 production) **significantly outperformed 1 or 3 retrievals** on all three posttests. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1164199)
- [RCT/Exp] Nakata & Webb (2016, *SSLA*): larger blocks (whole learning) lower in-session performance but help retention through **longer within-session spacing**; block size per se mattered once spacing was controlled. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271207356_Does_studying_vocabulary_in_smaller_sets_increase_learning_The_effects_of_part_and_whole_learning_on_second_language_vocabulary_acquisition)
- [Expert review] State-of-the-art review of distribution-of-practice effects on L2 learning (*Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching*). — [ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1365275.pdf); see also "Optimizing distributed practice online" (*SSLA*). — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/optimizing-distributed-practice-online/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)
- [RCT/Exp, binge vs spaced media] Binge-watching vs spaced viewing of captioned TV for learning L2 multi-word units was studied (spacing manipulation in media). — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09571736.2023.2211614)
- [Exp, single subject] Murre & Dros (2015, *PLoS ONE*) replicated Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve (savings method, ~70 h of learning; retention intervals 20 min–31 days); the curve **largely replicates but shows an upward jump at ~24 h** (plausibly sleep). Reported savings: 47.2% at 20 min, 31.7% at 1 day, 4.1% at 31 days [snippet-only; the 31-day figure looks low relative to Ebbinghaus's original and should be verified]. — [UvA DARE](https://dare.uva.nl/id/2deae194-9200-43ba-bf69-6ac797371502); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/1eb262a059254c17b017e217aee289d0)
- [Expert/docs] FSRS-6 makes the forgetting curve's flatness a per-user trainable parameter. — [fsrs-optimizer](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-optimizer)

### Inferences
- First review of new words is best placed after a night's sleep (next day), with within-session recycling on the day of learning.
- Session design: introduce a modest number of new words, but recycle each 5+ times within the session with other items in between (longer within-session lags), rather than drilling one word to criterion.
- Many short sessions across days (which streak mechanics already encourage) are aligned with the spacing evidence; a single long weekly binge is not.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis on optimal session *length* (minutes) for app-based L2 learning was found.
- No evidence retrieved on whether the timing of an evening session relative to sleep (e.g., bedtime review) adds benefit in app settings.
- Murre & Dros exact savings values are unverified.

## Q5. Errorful learning, hypercorrection, feedback timing (immediate vs delayed) for language items

### Takeaway
Making a guess before being taught (pretesting / errorful generation) followed by corrective feedback improves memory compared with errorless study, and confidently-held errors are especially well corrected (hypercorrection). For language drills, immediate and delayed explicit feedback are mostly similar, with a small edge for immediate feedback on vocabulary; immediate feedback appears to amplify spacing effects in L2. Feedback itself, rather than its exact timing, is what matters.

### Cited Findings
- [Expert/Annual Review] Metcalfe (2017, *Annual Review of Psychology*) "Learning from errors": errors followed by corrective feedback support learning; **high-confidence errors are hypercorrected**. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Learning%20from%20errorsAnnual%20ReviewMetcalfe2016.pdf)
- [RCT/Exp, classroom] Hypercorrection of high-confidence errors replicated in a classroom setting. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325252938_Hypercorrection_of_high-confidence_errors_in_the_classroom)
- [Expert review] *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (2021) "Unraveling the benefits of experiencing errors during learning": definition, modulating factors and theories of errorful learning. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-021-02022-8)
- [Expert/Exp] Pan et al. (2020): students and instructors underrate learning from errors; pretests/practice questions before content benefit learning. — [UCLA PDF](http://stevencpan.bol.ucla.edu/pdf/PSSCK_2020W.pdf); Zhang & Fiorella integrated model of learning from errors — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10429504)
- [RCT/Exp] Pretesting effect with word pairs (2025, *Journal of Cognition*): **immediate feedback was more effective than delayed feedback**, but **the pretesting benefit persisted with delayed feedback and delayed final tests**. — [PMC12292081](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12292081/); [J. Cognition PDF](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/455/files/687f6aa6dcf5d.pdf)
- [RCT/Exp] Pretesting effect is **robust throughout adulthood**, though metacognitive beliefs about pretesting/challenge differ by age (2025). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608025000597); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10620790)
- [RCT/Exp] Nakata (2015) feedback timing in L2 flashcard learning ("Does delaying feedback increase learning?"): collapsed across retrieval-frequency groups, only a **very small effect (d = 0.14) favouring immediate feedback** on the 1-week delayed receptive posttest [snippet-only]. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265381905_Effects_of_feedback_timing_on_second_language_vocabulary_learning_Does_delaying_feedback_increase_learning)
- [Expert/systematic review, 2025] "The timing of corrective feedback in second language learning" (*Language Teaching*): among studies with **explicit** feedback most found **no difference** between immediate and delayed CF; studies with **hybrid** CF tend to favour immediate; immediate CF during task performance generally beats CF delayed until after a communicative task. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/timing-of-corrective-feedback-in-second-language-learning/0E8856852D0183E9DD91EDB4C249E245); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388432318_The_timing_of_corrective_feedback_in_second_language_learning)
- [RCT/Exp] One L2 study reported immediate CF mean d = 1.34 vs delayed CF mean d = 0.73 [snippet-only; attribution to a specific paper unclear in the summary]. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344563296_THE_EFFECTS_OF_IMMEDIATE_AND_DELAYED_CORRECTIVE_FEEDBACK_ON_L2_DEVELOPMENT)
- [RCT/Exp] "Optimal timing of treatment for errors in second language learning" (PMC, 2023). — [PMC9995700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9995700/)
- [MA] Li (2010, *Language Learning*) meta-analysis of corrective feedback in SLA [effect sizes not in snippet]. — [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/2911659/Li_S_2010_The_effectiveness_of_corrective_feedback_in_SLA_A_meta_analysis_Language_Learning_60_309_365)
- [MA] Kim & Webb (2022): immediate feedback condition associated with large spacing effects (g = 1.04). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358406370_The_Effects_of_Spaced_Practice_on_Second_Language_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis)
- [MA] Rowland (2014): testing effects larger when feedback is provided. — [Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1727423/full)
- [RCT/Exp] Nakata & Suzuki (2019): interleaving produced **more training errors but better delayed retention** — an example where in-session errors are not a sign of poor learning. — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:7096acb5-f759-4fe9-a06a-d6a0e20f75d0/files/ssf268575c)

### Inferences
- **[Superseded for typical learners]** "Errorless learning is best" (from memory-impaired populations) does not generalize to healthy learners when corrective feedback follows; guessing first is beneficial.
- For app drills, show the correct answer immediately after an error (with audio) and re-queue the item later in the same session; this captures both the feedback benefit and within-session spacing.
- Collecting a quick confidence signal ("sure / not sure") enables hypercorrection-targeted re-queuing and gives the scheduler a better grade.
- In-session accuracy is a poor proxy for learning when desirable difficulties are in play; dashboards and difficulty tuning should use delayed recall.

### Gaps
- No retrieved evidence on hypercorrection or pretesting in children under ~10 or in L2 vocabulary specifically for children.
- Li (2010) and other CF meta-analytic effect sizes were not visible in snippets.
- No evidence retrieved on audio/pronunciation feedback timing.

## Implications for Hevalo

Concrete design implications drawn from the findings above (not from inspecting the codebase). Where a point rests on inference rather than direct evidence it says so.

**1. One memory model for every word, letter and grammar pattern, fed by every feature**
- Create a per-learner item-state store keyed by (user, item, skill). Items: Kurmanji lexemes (Hawar Latin), Sorani lexemes (Arabic-based script) as separate items, alphabet letters/sounds, and grammar patterns (e.g., ezafe forms, case endings, Kurmanji past-tense ergative agreement, Sorani pronominal clitics). Skills: meaning recognition (Kurdish→known language), **production (known language→Kurdish, typed)**, listening (audio→meaning), spelling/form. Evidence: productive retrieval transfers to receptive but not vice versa (Nakata), so production deserves its own item.
- Log a review row for every graded retrieval anywhere in the product (lessons, alphabet module, Wordle, rhyme rounds, typing race, quiz, Wordle battle, rhyme match, Kahoot-style multiplayer): user_id, item_id, skill, source feature, timestamp (ms), correct/incorrect, latency (ms), response format (MCQ / tap / typed / audio), hint used, derived 1–4 grade, state, duration. This is a superset of what FSRS's optimizer needs (card_id, review_time, rating 1–4, state, duration). Postgres for the log; Redis for per-user "due now" sorted sets.
- Log passive exposures separately (word appears in a community story the learner read, dictionary lookup, word heard in a game but not answered). Do not count them as FSRS reviews; use them as a feature or a small due-date nudge (inference; no published method exists).

**2. Scheduler: FSRS-6 now, a cross-user model later**
- Use FSRS-6 (open-source implementations are listed in awesome-fsrs) with default parameters for new users; run per-user optimization once a learner has ~1,000 reviews (pretrain possible from far fewer). Replace any Leitner/SM-2-style or fixed-interval review (SM-2 and HLR are clearly worse at prediction on the open benchmark).
- Format-aware grade mapping (inference): typed production correct + fast → Good/Easy; correct but slow, with hint, or after a typo-tolerant accept → Hard; wrong → Again; multiple-choice correct → at most Good, never Easy (recognition with guessing is weak evidence). Calibrate the mapping against Hevalo's own logs (log loss / RMSE of predicted vs actual next-review recall).
- Once Hevalo has a few hundred thousand reviews, evaluate a population model across users (the RWKV result shows cross-user training beats per-user fitting) or a mixed-effects HLR with lexeme features (2026 Duolingo-data paper), which suits a language app where word properties (frequency, length, cognate status with Arabic/Persian/Turkish, dialect) predict difficulty.
- Track prediction calibration and 7-day/30-day delayed recall as product KPIs, not only XP and streaks.

**3. Retention target and review load**
- Default desired retention ~0.90 for adult learners; offer a "relaxed" ~0.80–0.85 mode (default for children and casual users) and never above ~0.95 (workload rises steeply above 0.90 and is overwhelming above 0.97).
- Use the FSRS simulator approach to show each learner the projected daily review minutes for their target and to **cap new items per day** so review debt stays manageable; when reviews pile up, pause new lessons on the course map rather than letting the backlog grow.
- Make due reviews earn XP, streak credit and league points on equal footing with new lessons, so gamification rewards the behaviour with the largest evidence base (Duolingo's HLR A/B: +12% overall activity).

**4. Lesson/session structure on the course map**
- New items: guess first (pretest, e.g., picture or cognate guess), then immediate corrective feedback with recorded audio; then **5–7 retrievals per new word spread across the session** with other items in between (Nakata 2017; Nakata & Webb 2016), ending with the learner's own mistakes recycled (as Duolingo does).
- Move from recognition to production within the first session: tap/MCQ → word-bank build → **typed recall** with an on-screen helper for ê î û ç ş (Kurmanji) and Sorani letters; accept near-misses but mark them "Hard" and show the exact correct form.
- First review the next day (after sleep), then let FSRS expand. Avoid placing a "review" of the same words a few minutes after the lesson as if it were spaced practice.
- Prefer several short sessions over long ones (streak reminders already push this); for children, keep sessions short and frequent (inference from spacing + session-count moderator).

**5. Interleaving where it helps, blocking where it helps**
- Interleave **confusable** material: similar letters and diacritics (e/ê, i/î, u/û; Sorani letters differing by dots or vowel marks; Kurmanji vs Sorani forms of the same word for learners doing both), minimal-pair listening, and grammar contrasts (present vs past ergative constructions, ezafe by gender/number). Evidence: interleaving gains are largest for highly similar categories and for L2 grammar (Nakata & Suzuki 2019).
- Do not force interleaving for brand-new unrelated vocabulary (blocking beat interleaving for word materials, g = −0.39). Expect more errors during interleaved practice and do not let difficulty-tuning "fix" them away.

**6. Games and multiplayer as retrieval practice**
- Seed single-player and Kahoot-style multiplayer question pools from each player's due or nearly-due items (and from the group's shared course stage), so games double as spaced reviews; write every answer back to the memory model.
- Wordle and typing race are production tasks; give them production-skill credit. Rhyme rounds/match exercise phonological form; log under listening/form skills.
- After each game round, show the correct answers with audio (feedback strengthens testing effects); add an optional one-tap confidence ("sure?") in quiz modes and re-queue confident errors sooner (hypercorrection).

**7. Dictionary and community feed as the bridge to reading**
- "Save word" in the 400k-word dictionary and tap-to-translate in community stories/poems should create an item with its original sentence as context, entering the FSRS queue (mining workflow). Each later encounter in feed content is logged as an exposure.
- Recommend feed stories whose unknown-word load is low and that reuse the learner's due words, turning incidental encounters (r ≈ .34 with learning) into spaced re-exposure (inference).

**8. Learner-group specifics**
- **Heritage learners (diaspora)** often understand spoken Kurdish but not the written standard: give them a placement pretest so known items start with high stability (fewer wasted reviews), and track listening, meaning and spelling as separate skills so the scheduler focuses on written form and production.
- **Learners schooled in another language (Arabic/Turkish/Persian)**: cognates and script transfer should be item features for an HLR-style model later (inference).
- **Children**: the app must own the schedule (young children lack accurate beliefs about spacing); use retrieval with immediate feedback, short frequent sessions, a lower retention target, and a gentle "learn in the evening, quick check in the morning" family routine. Children appear to gain at least as much from sleep consolidation as adults.
- **Non-Kurds starting from zero**: alphabet module first, with interleaved letter-sound discrimination and recorded audio, then FSRS-scheduled letter and word reviews.

**9. Measuring whether it works**
- Run A/B tests the way Duolingo did for HLR: compare the scheduler/grade-mapping variants on delayed recall (spot-check items 7–30 days later), daily retention and review minutes per retained word, not on in-session accuracy (which desirable difficulties lower).
