# Verification of memory_retention.md (spaced repetition, retrieval practice, desirable difficulties)

> **How this check was done (read first).** The shared WebSearch budget for this run (200 calls) was already used up when verification began, so this check made **no new web searches**. I did not work around the limit. Three kinds of evidence were used instead:
> 1. **A primary source on disk.** Earlier in this session an agent cloned `github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark` into the scratchpad (commit `bd9110f`, 18 Sep 2026; `git fsck` clean). I read its README, model code and per-user result files directly and recomputed the means. Benchmark verdicts below rest on this source, not on snippets.
> 2. **Raw WebSearch results saved earlier in this session** by the research agents (memory_retention, sla_theory, children and others). These are the same kind of search-engine summary the notes were built from. They help where a *different* agent's search contradicts or adds to the notes (for example Kim & Webb, Goossens). They cannot independently confirm a snippet the notes already used.
> 3. **The fact-checker's background knowledge.** It is labelled "[background, unverified]" every time and is never used to mark a claim "confirmed".
>
> Verdict rule: "confirmed" only when a primary source was read. When the only support is the same search snippet the notes used, the verdict is "unverified", however plausible the claim is.

## Omitted counter-evidence and caveats

1. **Prediction benchmarks are not evidence of learning, and the baselines are humbling.** In the benchmark read locally, a **zero-parameter moving average of the user's recent success beats FSRS-6** on log loss (0.3369 vs 0.3460) and RMSE(bins) (0.0591 vs 0.0653). FSRS-6 wins only on AUC (0.7034 vs 0.7001). **HLR (0.4694) and SM-2 do worse than a constant** (AVG 0.3945). With same-day reviews evaluated, **FSRS-6 is also worse than the constant**. The notes do say benchmark wins concern prediction. They do not show how small FSRS-6's edge over trivial baselines is, or how weak it is on same-day reviews.
2. **The benchmark's HLR is not Duolingo's HLR.** `models/hlr.py` uses 3 parameters (two count features plus a bias) and **no lexeme or tag features**. Duolingo's version used those features. "HLR is superseded by FSRS on the open benchmark" is therefore not a fair test of a feature-rich, language-aware HLR, which is the variant the notes later recommend considering.
3. **Pass/fail grading costs little; skipping per-user fitting costs a lot.** FSRS-6 trained on **binary** grades scores log loss 0.3509 against 0.3460 with four grades. Default parameters score 0.3664. For an auto-graded app, the elaborate format-aware Hard/Good/Easy mapping in Implication 2 is worth much less than early per-user (or per-cohort) fitting and calibration checks. Treat it as optional.
4. **The benchmark population does not resemble Hevalo's users.** It is self-selected adult Anki users who **self-grade** flashcards. Default parameters and model rankings may not carry over to children, heritage speakers, or auto-graded game answers. Hevalo will need its own calibration monitoring from day one.
5. **Classroom null results for children's vocabulary (Goossens et al.)** found no benefit of retrieval practice or long-lag repetition in grades 2–6 vocabulary lessons. This is omitted from the notes (see row 2). Source is snippet-only, via the children agent's search: https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/7/20-1 ; https://repub.eur.nl/pub/77635/Proefschrift-Nicole-Goossens.pdf
6. **"Retrieval beats restudy by g ≈ 0.50–0.61" mixes comparisons.** The notes' own snippet says Adesope et al.'s 0.61 is against "other learning conditions", not restudy specifically. Against restudy, both Rowland (0.50) and, [background, unverified], Adesope's restudy subgroup (about 0.5) give roughly half a standard deviation. The 0.61 should not be quoted as a restudy contrast.
7. **"Guess first" for brand-new words is weaker than the notes imply.** [background, unverified] Errorful-generation and pretesting benefits come mostly from semantically related or guessable cue–target pairs. Huelser & Metcalfe (2012) found the benefit for related pairs but not unrelated ones. Kurdish word forms are arbitrary to a non-speaker, except for cognates. The pretesting result the notes cite (PMC12292081) used English word pairs. "Pretest every new word" should be limited to guessable items (cognates, pictures, morphology) or A/B-tested.
8. **Hypercorrection needs high-confidence errors.** [background, unverified] Those are common for general-knowledge facts and rare for words a learner has never seen. A "sure?" tap adds friction, especially for children, and its benefit for L2 vocabulary has not been shown. Its stronger use is as a grading signal, not as a learning intervention.
9. **Desirable difficulties can become undesirable.** [background, unverified] Rowland (2014) reported that without feedback, testing effects depend on reasonable initial retrieval success. Learners, especially children, also rate interleaving and spacing as *less* effective (for example Kornell & Bjork 2008), which risks churn. The notes' own Vlach et al. (2019) points the same way. Hevalo should keep feedback on every retrieval, use scaffolds (word bank before typed recall), and watch dropout alongside delayed recall.
10. **Within-session repetition has diminishing returns.** [background, unverified] Rawson & Dunlosky's relearning studies found that retrievals spread across sessions matter more than drilling to a high criterion in one session. This argues for capping in-session retrievals (about 3–5) and moving repetitions to later days, rather than requiring 5–7 for every word.
11. **Smaller factual fixes.**
    - Ye, Su & Cao (KDD 2022) describe **220 million memory-behaviour logs**, not "220 million learners".
    - Murre & Dros's "4.1% savings at 31 days" stays unverified. [background, unverified] Ebbinghaus's original 31-day savings was about 21%, so this figure is suspicious but not product-critical.
    - The Kim & Webb (2023, *MLJ*) result on whether spacing works differently for fill-in-the-blanks vs flashcards is still unknown. It bears directly on whether game-style retrievals deserve the same scheduling as flashcards.
